CAG's decadal report on state finances: budget size matters less than what it buys

Economy / Fiscal FederalismRAS Mains · Public finance; fiscal federalism; state finances (Rajasthan relevance)

Question (10 marks, ~150 words):

'It is not the size of a state's budget but the quality and composition of its expenditure that determines growth.' Discuss in the light of the CAG's decadal analysis of state finances (2014-25), with reference to the fiscal challenges of states like Rajasthan.

Model Answer

Introduction:

  • The CAG's 'State Finances 2024-25: Decadal Analysis' (its third such report, covering all 28 states, 2014-25) finds states now collectively outspend the Union — making the composition of state budgets India's biggest fiscal-policy lever.

Size is not destiny:

  • Growth ran opposite to budget size: affluent small-budget states (13.1% of GSDP) grew 7%; poorer large-budget states (22.9%) grew 6.4%; medium-budget states trailed at 5.6%.
  • Own-tax/GSDP slipped 6.5% → 6.3% despite 11.1% post-GST growth — tax effort, not GST alone, remains the constraint.

Composition is:

  • General services (salaries, interest, pensions) rising from 3.7% to 6.8% of GSDP tracks growth falling 7% → 5.7% — committed liabilities crowd out productive spend.
  • Social and economic services investment by Bihar, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, MP, Odisha and Rajasthan aided convergence toward richer states.

Rajasthan's challenge:

  • Convergence gains coexist with OPS pension liabilities, a heavy debt-interest bill and salary commitments — precisely the CAG's weak-growth category; 15th FC-style tied and equalising transfers plus expenditure-quality rules (FRL review, capex protection) are the corrective.

Way forward:

  • Larger but conditioned transfers to poorer states; publish committed-expenditure ratios; protect capital outlay floors.

Conclusion:

  • Budgets buy growth only when they buy capability — schools, clinics, infrastructure — not an expanding salary-pension-interest bill.
