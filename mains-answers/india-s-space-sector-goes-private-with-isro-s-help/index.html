India's space sector goes private, with ISRO's help

Science & Technology / EconomyRAS Mains GS-III · Science & technology; space economy

Question (10 marks, ~150 words):

India's space sector is taking a private turn on the NASA–SpaceX model. Discuss its strategic and economic significance, and the risks of this shift.

Model Answer

Introduction: India's space sector is taking a NASA-SpaceX-style private turn, dramatised by Skyroot's Vikram-1, its first orbital test flight under 'Mission Aagaman', following the 2022 Vikram-S suborbital hop. The question demands assessing its strategic and economic significance alongside the risks.

Strategic and economic significance:

  • Strategically, reliable, frequent access to orbit is a capability only a handful of nations possess; globally only SpaceX and Rocket Lab launch to orbit regularly, so India's entry could break a bottleneck where satellite operators wait months or years for a slot.
  • The all-carbon, 3D-printed-engine Vikram-1 carries 350 kg to low Earth orbit and will capture in-flight data ground testing cannot replicate.
  • Economically, it could help grow the space economy from about $8.4 billion now to $44 billion by 2033, unlocking jobs, exports and downstream ventures, with reforms letting private firms operate ISRO's SSLV, PSLV and LVM-3 and co-build systems for ISRO's own missions.

Risks:

  • An unproven orbital rocket carries genuine technical risk until one or two flights succeed.
  • Over-reliance on a few startups could concentrate a strategically vital capability in private hands.

Way forward:

  • Prove Vikram-1 over one or two demonstration flights.
  • Deepen the co-build partnership, and widen the launcher base beyond a single firm.

Conclusion: If Vikram-1 reaches orbit, India joins a tiny club, provided reforms mature into a resilient, ISRO-anchored ecosystem.
