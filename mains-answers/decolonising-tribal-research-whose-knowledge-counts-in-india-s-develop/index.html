Decolonising tribal research: whose knowledge counts in India's development story?

Society / Tribal DevelopmentRAS Mains · Indian society; tribal communities & development; knowledge systems (Rajasthan relevance)

Question (10 marks, ~150 words):

Research about tribal communities has historically been an extractive, top-down exercise. In this light, discuss the significance of 'indigenous knowledge sovereignty' and examine how India's legal and policy framework can make tribal development more participatory.

Model Answer

Introduction:

  • Historically, knowledge about Adivasis was produced by outsiders who treated communities as objects of study; 'indigenous knowledge sovereignty' reasserts them as producers of knowledge with narrative sovereignty and relational accountability.

Why it matters:

  • Traditional Ecological Knowledge underpins forest, seed and water resilience — a climate asset.
  • Oral traditions and mother tongues (e.g., Bhili, Wagdi) encode ecology, medicine and cosmology; language loss is knowledge loss.
  • Extractive research yields development that fails on the ground — displacement, broken rehabilitation.

Frameworks:

  • Ecological Memory Mapping, Decolonial Ethnography, Language Revitalisation & Storywork, Emancipatory Research, Digital Decolonisation — together a pluriversal, praxis-oriented model.

India's legal-policy anchors:

  • Forest Rights Act, 2006 and PESA, 1996 vest the Gram Sabha with authority over community forest resources; Fifth Schedule, Tribal Sub-Plan, TRIFED and Van Dhan provide institutional routes.

Rajasthan application:

  • The Banswara–Dungarpur–Udaipur TSP belt (Bhil, Mina, Garasia, Sahariya PVTG) needs mother-tongue documentation and Gram Sabha-routed, co-designed schemes.

Way forward & conclusion:

  • Co-produced, consent-based research with guard-rails against romanticism and elite capture — development done with Adivasis, not to them.
