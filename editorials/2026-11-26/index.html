Polity & ConstitutionGS-II · Constitution, Judiciary, Basic structure · RAS Mains Paper II (Indian Polity)

Constitution Day: A Living Document and the Promise of Timely Justice

Why in news: India marks Constitution Day on 26 November, 77 years after the Constituent Assembly adopted the Constitution. On the eve, CJI Surya Kant called the Constitution a living document that courts must read in the light of social, technological and global change, always within the basic structure. He said the judiciary must introspect and deliver justice that is timely and affordable, and must find answers to new threats such as 'digital arrest' frauds and AI. In an Indian Express article, the Prime Minister recalled Granville Austin's description of the Constitution as 'first and foremost a social document'.

  • Adopted 26 November 1949; in force 26 January 1950; Constitution Day observed since 2015 (Ambedkar's 125th birth anniversary year)
  • Constituent Assembly: President Dr Rajendra Prasad; Drafting Committee chair Dr B.R. Ambedkar; about 2 years, 11 months and 18 days of work
  • Basic structure doctrine: Kesavananda Bharati (1973); elements named by the CJI — rule of law, democracy, fundamental rights, independent judiciary
  • Pendency (NJDG, July 2026): Supreme Court about 95,936 cases; High Courts about 64.67 lakh; district courts about 4.98 crore
  • High Courts: 309 of 1,122 sanctioned judge posts vacant (Feb 2026); Rajasthan High Court had about 6.88 lakh pending cases (Dec 2025)
  • Article 39A (free legal aid) and Article 21 (speedy trial, Hussainara Khatoon, 1979) make timely justice a constitutional duty

The Constitution has survived because it can change without breaking. Article 368 allows amendment, but the basic structure doctrine stops Parliament from destroying its core. Courts have widened rights through interpretation — privacy as part of Article 21 (Puttaswamy, 2017), the right to a clean environment, and the right to internet access for education and trade (Anuradha Bhasin, 2020). The CJI's point is that the same method must now answer new questions: who protects a citizen duped by a 'digital arrest' call, how evidence produced or altered by AI is tested, and how cross-border online disputes are heard. A living Constitution, however, is only as real as the courtroom that applies it; a right delayed for years is, for most citizens, a right denied.

Economy & TechnologyGS-III · Investment, Employment, Infrastructure, AI · RAS Mains Paper II (Economy)

The Data Centre Trap: Big Investment, Little Domestic Value

Why in news: As US communities push back against AI data centres, operators are looking for new hosts abroad, and India is courting them with incentives. In an Indian Express op-ed, economist Sajjid Chinoy argues that India should not become a hub for 'exporting compute', because data centres are highly capital- and import-intensive, create few jobs and do little for the balance of payments.

  • An $8 billion AI data centre creates about 100 permanent jobs after construction
  • The same $8 billion elsewhere in India's economy is typically linked to about 400,000 jobs (capital stock per worker ≈ $20,000)
  • So AI data centres are roughly 4,000 times more capital-intensive than the rest of the economy
  • Over 25 years capital contributed about 4 percentage points to annual growth versus 1.7 for labour
  • In Malaysia, Asia's hottest data-centre hub, the BoP improved little because of tech imports and profit repatriation

High-end chips and servers are imported, so most of the investment leaks abroad. Data centres are largely foreign-owned, so profits are repatriated. Very few jobs are created once construction ends. The 'export compute' model therefore adds little domestic value while using scarce land, power and water. By comparative advantage, India should import capital-intensive compute and export labour-intensive goods and services. Even 'sovereign' compute rests on imported chips that can be cut off at short notice, as US restrictions on chip imports by non-US firms in South-East Asia showed.

Environment & Climate DiplomacyGS-III · Climate change, Energy · GS-II · International agreements · RAS Mains Paper III

After COP31: Why India Wants a Nationally Determined Energy Transition

Why in news: COP31 at Antalya ended without its main political declaration: the host's 'Antalya Pact' left out a fossil-fuel roadmap and was rejected by the EU and others. In an Indian Express interview, Environment Minister Bhupender Yadav said India accepts the need for a faster shift to clean energy but opposes a uniform timetable for phasing out fossil fuels.

  • COP28 Dubai (2023) agreed on 'transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems'
  • COP30 Belém (2025) ended without a roadmap despite backing from more than 80 countries; the presidency said a roadmap would come to COP31
  • At COP31 oil-producing Arab states again opposed transition roadmaps
  • India's updated NDC: 50% of installed power capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030 (achieved in 2025); net zero by 2070
  • Coal still supplies over 70% of India's electricity generation
