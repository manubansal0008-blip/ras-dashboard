Treat Air Pollution Like a Pandemic: Six Lessons from COVID-19

Why in news: As north India enters its peak smog season, public-health experts argue that air pollution, which harms the same organs as COVID-19 through the same breath, still gets only seasonal attention.

COVID-19 was declared a public health emergency and met with a national, all-government response. Air pollution, a year-round killer, is still treated as a seasonal nuisance. An adult takes 20,000–25,000 breaths a day, so even a small worsening of air quality adds up to a large health burden over time.

  • Over 2 million deaths in India in 2023 linked to air pollution (State of Global Air 2025)
  • Economic loss ≈ 1.36% of India's GDP in 2019 (Lancet Planetary Health study)
  • National Clean Air Programme (NCAP) launched in 2019
  • GRAP: emergency curbs during severe pollution episodes in Delhi-NCR
  • Rajasthan's NCAP non-attainment cities include Jaipur, Jodhpur, Kota, Alwar and Udaipur

The lessons are clear. First, political leadership and coordination across ministries and State boundaries. Second, health surveillance: AQI readings should be linked with data on respiratory emergencies, OPD visits and deaths. Third, money: NCAP needs mission-mode funding and outcome targets measured in reduced exposure, not in the number of restrictions. Fourth, regional partnerships, since pollution crosses borders. Fifth, separate emergency measures like GRAP from year-round prevention through clean transport, industrial compliance and waste management. Sixth, tighter air-quality standards closer to WHO guidelines.

Rajasthan angle: the State faces desert dust, brick kilns, mining and fast-growing urban traffic; industrial Bhiwadi is often among India's most polluted towns, and five cities are under NCAP. Linking AQI with hospital data in Jaipur, Jodhpur and Kota, greening along highways and controlling construction dust would give quick gains.
