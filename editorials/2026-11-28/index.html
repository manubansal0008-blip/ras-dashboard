Polity & FederalismGS-II · Parliament, Federalism, Constitutional amendments · RAS Mains Paper II (Indian Polity)

Delimitation: Balancing 'One Person, One Vote' with Federalism

Why in news: The Centre will bring revised Bills in a special sitting from 30 November to increase Lok Sabha and Assembly seats by 50%, extend the inter-State freeze on seat shares till 2051, redraw boundaries on the 2011 Census and bring in 33% reservation for women before 2029. The Hindu's editorial ('Fair play') calls the substance a reasonable way to balance democracy and federalism but criticises the hurried process; The Indian Express argues the session is really about delimitation rather than women.

  • Arts. 81, 82, 170: composition of Lok Sabha/Assemblies and readjustment after each census
  • Delimitation Commissions: 1952, 1963, 1973 (on 1971 Census), 2002 (intra-State, on 2001 Census)
  • 42nd Amendment (1976): froze inter-State seat shares on the 1971 Census till 2001, to reward population control
  • 84th Amendment (2001): extended the freeze till the first census after 2026; 87th (2003) used the 2001 Census for boundaries
  • 106th Amendment (2023): 33% women's reservation, to start after delimitation following the first census after its commencement
  • Population has grown at least 2.6 times since 1971; Lok Sabha could rise to about 815 (max 850)

Strict 'one person, one vote' would shift seats from southern and smaller States that controlled population to the populous north. A uniform 50% rise keeps every State's share intact, protecting the group rights of States, while a bigger House narrows the gap in the number of people per MP. But it also means that the representation gap between, say, a Kerala and a Uttar Pradesh voter is frozen for another generation. The Hindu argues that on a question this central to federal democracy, the process matters as much as the formula: consensus was built in 1976 and 2001 under Prime Ministers of different parties, and the Bills should now go to a parliamentary committee rather than be pushed through a four-day sitting.

International Relations — NeighbourhoodGS-II · India and its neighbourhood · RAS Mains Paper II (International Relations)

Balen Shah's Visit: A Delivery-First Model for Neighbourhood First

Why in news: In The Hindu, Constantino Xavier (CSEP) argues that Nepal PM Balendra Shah's first bilateral visit to New Delhi on 23 November opened a new, pragmatic chapter. The 36-year-old former rapper and Kathmandu mayor, who came to power after Nepal's Gen Z uprising, held a two-hour private meeting with PM Modi and returned with a large economic package. The visit was short and work-focused — no banquets, little talk of civilisational ties, and silence on old irritants — and the author suggests it could be a model for reviving ties with Bangladesh.

  • Shah took office on 27 March 2026 after the Gen Z protests and elections; this was his first bilateral visit abroad
  • Before the visit, the sides prepared a 10-point joint vision based on 'Samriddha Nepal, Samunnat Bharat'
  • A trial run of the Ayodhya–Janakpur rail service was held on 5 November 2026
  • India gave support after the Rasuwa–Bhotekoshi floods
  • Static: 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship; open border of about 1,751 km; India is Nepal's largest trade partner and the main transit route

The author's central point is that pragmatism beat pageantry. Rather than playing the victim or using China as leverage, Shah chose to visit New Delhi first and treat India as Nepal's indispensable economic partner. New Delhi, in turn, kept old irritants — the boundary at Kalapani–Lipulekh, the 1950 Treaty, and past blockades — off the table and focused on economic connectivity: rail, power trade, transit and investment. This 'delivery-oriented partnership' gives a young, aspirational electorate visible results, which is the best antidote to anti-India sentiment. The same model — economic connectivity first, quiet handling of disputes — could help reset ties with Bangladesh.
