Economy & Social JusticeRAS Mains · Indian Economy — poverty, unemployment, inclusive growth

Beyond the Poverty Line: India's Next Challenge Is Jobs and Wages

Why in news: The World Bank's latest Poverty & Equity Brief says extreme poverty in India fell from 27.1% in 2011 to 2.6% in 2023 at the $3-a-day line. Editorials welcomed the progress but warned that stagnant real wages, youth unemployment and regional concentration of the poor now define the problem.

India has crossed a milestone: almost no one now lives on less than $3 a day. But a large group lives just above the line, and a small shock — illness, crop loss or a job loss — can push them back. The question has shifted from survival to security and mobility.

  • Extreme poverty ($3/day): 27.1% (2011) → 2.6% (2023)
  • At $4.20/day: 57.7% → 16.1%; rural 19.3% vs urban 8.6%
  • Consumption Gini: 28.8 (2011-12) → 23.0 (2023), though surveys under-capture the rich
  • About 57% of the remaining poor live in a belt from Uttar Pradesh to West Bengal
  • Employment rose by about 13.4 crore (2017–2023), led by rural women, but real earnings stagnated

Way forward: the next phase needs productive jobs, not only transfers — labour-intensive manufacturing and MSME clusters in the eastern belt, skilling linked to industry, safer and better-paid work for women, and fresh survey frames from the coming Census so that policy targets the right places.

Rajasthan angle: the state's own priorities — rural employment, women's self-help groups under Rajeevika, and MSME promotion — fit this shift from relief to livelihoods.

Social Justice & LabourRAS Mains · Welfare schemes, labour reforms, vulnerable sections

A Board Is Not a Benefit: Making Social Security Real for Gig Workers

Why in news: On 30 October 2026 the Ministry of Labour and Employment notified the National Social Security Board for unorganised workers and gig and platform workers under the Code on Social Security, 2020. An editorial argued that boards alone have produced suboptimal outcomes so far — workers need schemes, funds and easy enrolment.

Gig and platform work — delivery, ride-hailing, home services — is among the fastest-growing kinds of work in Indian cities. Workers are treated as 'partners', not employees, so they lack the PF, ESI and leave that regular workers get. The Code on Social Security, 2020 was the first law to recognise them by name.

  • Board notified: 30 Oct 2026; chaired by the Union Labour Minister; three-year term; meets at least thrice a year
  • For gig matters: 5 aggregator and 5 worker representatives, plus ESIC, EPFO, States and experts
  • Section 113: registration of gig/platform workers (age 16+), including self-registration online
  • Section 114: schemes for life and disability cover, accident insurance, health, maternity, old age and crèche
  • Aggregators to contribute 1–2% of annual turnover, capped at 5% of amounts paid to gig workers

Way forward: notify concrete schemes with timelines; collect the aggregator contribution through a simple, transaction-linked system; enrol workers automatically using platform data; publish how the fund is spent; and give workers a quick grievance channel.

Rajasthan angle: Rajasthan was the first state to pass a law for platform-based gig workers (2023). Its experience with a welfare cess on platforms is directly relevant to the national scheme design.

Mains question (10 marks): The constitution of the National Social Security Board is a necessary but not sufficient step towards protecting gig and platform workers. Examine.

Environment & GovernanceRAS Mains · Environment — water pollution, river conservation, governance

What the Rhine Can Teach the Yamuna

Why in news: An opinion piece argued that the Rhine's recovery after the 1986 chemical disaster holds lessons for the Yamuna, which stays heavily polluted in Delhi despite decades of spending and several action plans.

In November 1986 a fire at the Sandoz chemical warehouse near Basel (Switzerland) washed tonnes of pesticides into the Rhine and killed fish for hundreds of kilometres. The disaster pushed the countries along the river to act together, with clear targets and shared monitoring.

  • International Commission for the Protection of the Rhine (ICPR), set up in 1950, coordinates riparian countries
  • Rhine Action Programme (1987) set the target of bringing salmon back to the river
  • Yamuna: the Delhi stretch is a small part of the river's length but carries most of its pollution load
  • Yamuna Action Plan began in 1993; spending has continued under several later programmes
  • Untreated sewage from drains such as Najafgarh is the main source of pollution in Delhi

Way forward: one authority answerable for the whole river; full sewage collection and treatment in Delhi; guaranteed minimum environmental flow from upstream states; and monthly public reporting of water quality against fixed targets.

Rajasthan angle: the same lessons apply to the state's polluted rivers — the Supreme Court is currently monitoring the Jojari–Luni–Bandi system, where industrial effluent from Jodhpur, Pali and Balotra is the main problem.

Mains question (10 marks): Despite decades of action plans, river cleaning in India has shown limited results. What lessons can India draw from international experiences such as the Rhine? Discuss with reference to the Yamuna.
